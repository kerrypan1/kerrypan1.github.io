Overview

I used PixNerd to denoise, generate, and edit images, then trained my own flow-matching UNets on MNIST.

Part A: Exploring Flow Models

Model: PixNerd-XXL-P16-T2I at 512×512. Default prompt: “a high quality photo.” Euler step size: 0.02. All guided runs use w = 7 and an empty unconditional prompt.

1.1: Constructing Noisy Inputs

I used this gorilla photo and one Gaussian noise tensor (seed 180), reused across all three times and the denoising comparisons.

1.2: Classical Denoising

After rescaling by 1/t, I tried blur strengths σ = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and chose the lowest MSE against the original. The fur loses detail, especially at t = 0.25.

1.3: One-Step Denoising

One velocity prediction recovers the face much better than Gaussian blur, though the fur is still smoothed out at lower t.

1.4: Visualizing Velocity

The predicted and true updates share one symmetric scale, with zero at middle gray. Error is RGB RMS before clipping, on a shared linear scale. Much of the visible error follows the face and fur details.

1.5: Iterative Denoising

I took 25 Euler steps from the saved t = 0.50 input. Below are the initial state and every fifth update. Constant-velocity checks passed for 50 full steps, 25 half-path steps, and a shortened final step from t = 0.93.

Comparing the methods

On the same noisy input, Euler brings back sharper fur, but one-step denoising stays closer to the original by MSE: blur 0.01915, one step 0.01173, Euler 0.01482. Sharper texture does not necessarily mean a more accurate reconstruction.

1.6: Generating Images from Noise

With “a high quality photo,” all five samples are human portraits, even though the prompt never mentions people. This suggests a bias toward portraits for this prompt, at least across these seeds. Some faces have missing features or distorted proportions.

2.1: Classifier-Free Guidance

Using the same five noise tensors, CFG at w = 7 gives sharper faces and fixes many of the missing or distorted features. The results fit “a high quality photo” better, although that prompt is too broad to judge subject alignment. They also look more alike, with smooth skin, dark backgrounds, and studio-style lighting, so the improvement comes with less visual variety. The w = 0 check matched ordinary conditional sampling.

Part B: Flow Matching from Scratch

Training uses the 60,000 MNIST training images, shuffled each epoch, with fresh noise per batch. Denoiser evaluations use the separate test set. Pixels are in [0, 1].

1.1: Implementing the UNet

I implemented the specified convolutional UNet with batch normalization, GELU, and skip connections. Hidden width is 128 for denoising and 64 for flow matching.

1.2: Noising MNIST

The same training digit at all seven noise levels:

1.2.1: Training a Denoiser

Five epochs, σ = 0.5, batch size 256, Adam at 10−4, and MSE loss. Most digits are already readable after one epoch; the fifth-epoch outputs have cleaner strokes. Rows are clean / noisy / denoised, with the same test inputs at both checkpoints.

1.2.2: Out-of-Distribution Testing

I kept the test digit and noise tensor fixed while varying σ. The 7 stays recognizable through σ = 0.6, but gains extra strokes at 0.8 and 1.0. Top: noisy inputs. Bottom: denoised outputs.

1.2.3: Denoising Pure Noise

After both one and five epochs, different noise inputs produce almost the same blurry, loop-shaped digit. The noise is independent of the target image, so it gives the model no information about which digit to draw. Under MSE, the best prediction in that situation is the average MNIST image. Overlapping strokes from digits 0–9 explain the faint loops and blur.

2.1: Time Conditioning

Two fully connected time embeddings multiply the decoder features at 7×7 and 14×14. I train on interpolated noise–digit pairs to predict velocity, sampling t from a normalized 50-point grid.

2.2: Training the Time-Conditioned UNet

Ten epochs, batch size 64, Adam starting at 0.01, and velocity MSE. The learning rate decays by 0.1^(1/10) each epoch. The plot includes every optimizer step and a 100-step moving average.

2.3: Sampling with Time Conditioning

Starting from Gaussian noise, I used 50 Euler steps and seed 180 for each checkpoint. The first epoch produces broken strokes; later checkpoints produce more recognizable digits, with some ambiguous shapes remaining.

2.4: Class Conditioning

I added two one-hot class embeddings: class scales the decoder features and time adds a bias. Class conditioning is dropped for 10% of training examples.

2.5: Training the Class-Conditioned UNet

I used D = 64, batch size 64, Adam at 0.01, and 10 epochs. I trained two models from the same seed: one with exponential learning rate decay and one with a constant learning rate.

2.6: Class-Conditioned Sampling

I sampled four instances of each digit using 300 Euler steps, seed 180, and guidance v_u + 5(v_c − v_u). Columns correspond to digits 0–9. Conditioning gives much more consistent digit identities than the time-only model.

Removing the scheduler

I removed the scheduler and kept Adam's learning rate fixed at 0.01 for all 10 epochs. The seed, architecture, batch size, and training length stayed the same. I made no further adjustment; this run still produced readable examples of all ten digits. Both loss curves fall quickly and then flatten at a similar level on the plotted scale. The final digits look comparable, with a few rough or uneven strokes in both runs.
